Resolving the Hermite coefficient conjectures in OEIS A381423, Comments (revision 8)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Where does the largest coefficient sit in a Hermite polynomial, and how does its position change as the degree increases? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang give an explicit answer: with signs retained, the winning exponent always rises by one or falls by three. They also describe the different pattern obtained when signs are ignored.

A polynomial is a sum of powers of a variable multiplied by coefficients. The question concerns which power carries the largest coefficient, not where the polynomial function is largest. The authors use the physicists’ normalization of Hermite polynomials: nonzero terms have exponents separated by two and alternating signs. For example, H₅(x) = 32x⁵ − 160x³ + 120x. The largest signed coefficient is 120, at exponent one; the largest absolute coefficient is 160, at exponent three. Such distinctions motivate Mike Sheppard’s conjectures in the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences, especially A381423, Comments, revision 8.

Let a(n) denote the winning signed exponent at degree n. Positive coefficients have exponents differing from n by multiples of four. The authors prove uniqueness and give a selection rule: a(n) is the smallest nonnegative integer j in that class satisfying j² + 7j + 7 ≥ 2n. Comparing an entire row of coefficients thus becomes solving a quadratic inequality and rounding within a prescribed class. Consequently, the winning exponent grows like √(2n), rather than proportionally to the degree.

The proof compares neighboring positive coefficients, moving downward through exponents four at a time. Cancelling factorials in the known coefficient formula produces ratios that strictly decrease. Hence the coefficients increase and then decrease, with the peak determined by where the ratio crosses one. An integer argument excludes equality: the comparison reduces to two integer squares with a fixed correction too small to bridge their possible gap. This yields both uniqueness and the quadratic threshold. Comparing thresholds at adjacent degrees shows that the number of four-step moves to the peak increases by zero or one, explaining the exponent changes of one or minus three.

The same threshold explains the larger pattern. Successive differences form blocks of three rises followed by a drop, or four rises followed by a drop; the first block is longer. Explicit floor-quadratic formulas locate every longer block. Their starting degrees grow quadratically with their enumeration, while the number of shorter blocks between consecutive longer ones grows linearly, up to bounded fluctuations. This makes the earlier informal spacing description precise: shorter blocks do not have one constant spacing.

Ignoring signs means comparing coefficients two exponents apart. If b(n) is the smallest exponent attaining the absolute maximum, it is the least nonnegative j of the same parity as n satisfying j² + 5j + 2 ≥ 2n. Its successive changes are one or minus one, arranged as one or two rises followed by a drop. Absolute maxima can tie, but only at two neighboring nonzero terms. The authors determine all tie degrees, beginning 8, 13, 34, 43 and 76, and derive their generating function and recurrence. This addresses Sheppard’s three formulas in A381737 and the conjecture in A381524. At degree eight, the tied exponents are two and four, with opposite signs; the signed winner is four.

The two winning exponents always differ by at most two. Although their winning values need not agree, the largest signed coefficient divided by the largest absolute coefficient tends to one. For either sequence of winning values, the next value divided by the current value is asymptotic to √(2n). These results extend the maximal-coefficient sequences recorded by V. Reshetnikov, rather than introducing the underlying Hermite coefficient formulas.

The manuscript supplies written proofs for these all-degree claims. It credits earlier Lean evidence only with characterizing the smallest exponent attaining the signed maximum and proving the one-or-minus-three assertion, not the other formulas or absolute results. Separately, the authors report exact-polynomial comparisons through degree 600, threshold and block checks through one million, and a tie generating-function check through coefficient 10,000. These finite checks neither replace the proofs nor constitute independent verification here. The claimed settlement concerns the specified coefficient-position questions in this normalization. Auxiliary Riordan, row-sum and related-array identities remain untreated, not established as open problems; the authors leave no unresolved case within their stated coefficient-mode claims.
